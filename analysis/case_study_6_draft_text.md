**Case Study 6: Biome-Specific Thermal Risk Heterogeneity in Brazil**

Populations living in chronically hotter climates may respond to temperature in one of two opposing ways: physiological and behavioural acclimatisation could blunt the marginal effect of heat on mortality, or accumulated exposure and co-morbidity burden could instead amplify it. International evidence already shows that the shape of the temperature–mortality relationship varies systematically with a location's climate and latitude — a large multi-country analysis found lower relative risks at extreme temperatures in populations from warmer, tropical settings than in temperate ones [17]. Brazil offers an unusually direct within-country test of this pattern: a single continental territory spanning roughly 35° of latitude and all five of the country's major biomes — Amazônia, Cerrado, Caatinga, Mata Atlântica, and Pampa — analysed here for the first time with one harmonised outcome definition, one modelling specification, and one climate exposure pipeline across every state.

**Methods.** We used the `dlnm_exposure_response` Gold-tier dataset from climasusDB v1.0.0, which reports a state-level distributed lag non-linear model (DLNM) of climate-sensitive mortality against temperature for all 27 Brazilian federative units (Methods; Data Records). Each state was assigned to its single dominant biome from a fixed lookup table; several states span more than one biome, so this assignment is an approximation, not a precise ecological classification. For each state we extracted its median temperature (the exposure value at the 50th percentile marker), its peak cumulative relative risk (RR) across the exposure curve, and the temperature at which that peak occurs, then summarised these by biome. We tested the association between state median temperature and peak RR using Spearman's rank correlation (n = 27), with a 95% confidence interval from 10,000 bootstrap resamples. This analysis is exploratory and descriptive; it is not designed to support a causal claim.

**Results.** Peak cumulative RR fell as biome median temperature rose: Pampa (median 19.8°C, one state) had the highest peak RR (1.58), followed by Mata Atlântica (median 22.0°C; peak RR median 1.29, IQR 1.22–1.38), Cerrado (25.2°C; 1.25, IQR 1.14–1.30), Amazônia (27.0°C; 1.19, IQR 1.12–1.23), and Caatinga (26.3°C; 1.03, IQR 1.01–1.10) — the lowest of the five. Across all 27 states, median temperature and peak RR were negatively correlated (Spearman's ρ = −0.66, p = 0.0002, 95% bootstrap CI −0.84 to −0.34), a result consistent with the acclimatisation hypothesis rather than the accumulated-vulnerability hypothesis (Figures A–B). A closer look, however, tempers a simple heat-acclimatisation reading: in 18 of the 27 states (67%), the peak RR is driven by an extreme *cold*, not hot, temperature — including 100% of the six Mata Atlântica states and the single Pampa state, and 83% of Amazônia's own states. The correlation is therefore better read as reflecting a mixture of two established phenomena operating along the same latitudinal gradient: comparatively higher cold-related risk in Brazil's temperate south, and comparatively lower heat-related risk in its tropical north — not a single acclimatisation process acting alone. Ceará (Caatinga; median 26.5°C) had the lowest peak RR (1.00), and Rio Grande do Sul (Pampa; median 19.8°C) the highest (1.58, driven by a cold extreme at 7.7°C).

**Caveats.** These are state-level models, a deliberate design choice given that most of Brazil's 5,570 municipalities record too few daily deaths for a stable municipality-level DLNM, not a limitation of the underlying mortality data. Twenty-two of the 27 state models (81%) show statistically significant residual autocorrelation, meaning their confidence intervals are likely too narrow; this affects every Mata Atlântica and Caatinga state and the majority of Cerrado and Amazônia states. Biome assignment used each state's single dominant biome only, an approximation for the several states that straddle more than one. The correlation is based on 27 observations and should not be over-interpreted, and — as shown above — a majority of states' peak-risk estimates reflect cold rather than heat extremes, so this analysis should not be read as a clean test of heat acclimatisation alone. This is nonetheless the first systematic, harmonised, within-country comparison of this kind for Brazil, not a definitive causal analysis of climate adaptation.

This case study illustrates what climasusDB makes possible: a national-scale, harmonised comparison across every Brazilian biome that was not previously feasible, because no single dataset had covered all 27 states with the same outcome definition, model specification, and climate exposure integration.
